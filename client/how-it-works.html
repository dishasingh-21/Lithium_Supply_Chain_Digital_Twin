Lithium Supply Chain Digital Twin

A simulation of the real supply chain behind every battery this Route Planner's EVs run on — from brine extraction in Chile to a finished pack on the road in Rajasthan.

Built on: EV Route Optimizer + Charging Station Network

A digital twin is not just another dashboard. A dashboard shows charts of what already happened. A digital twin is a running model that mirrors a physical system closely enough that you can ask it "what if this part of the chain breaks?" and trust the answer. That takes three things together: a state that stays in sync with the real system (actual inventory levels and shipment positions, not stale averages), behavior models that can predict what happens next (mass balance, inventory dynamics, disruption risk), and a feedback loop, so a decision made using the twin can actually change the real system — a scenario run here becomes a real procurement or routing decision.

Lithium for an EV battery crosses three continents and several industrial stages before it ever reaches a car in Rajasthan, and each stage is a real physical bottleneck, which is why a disruption anywhere in this chain is worth taking seriously. It starts as brine pumped from underground reservoirs beneath Chile's Atacama salt flats into shallow evaporation ponds, where sun and wind concentrate it over many months — a slow step, highly dependent on weather and water availability, so a drought here is a real, documented risk. The concentrated brine is then shipped and refined into battery-grade lithium carbonate or hydroxide, almost entirely in China, which processes roughly two-thirds of the world's lithium chemicals regardless of where it was mined (IEA Critical Minerals Report, 2022) — the single biggest geographic concentration risk in the whole chain. From there the refined lithium becomes cathode precursor material and is assembled into battery cells, still largely in China at the scale of CATL-class manufacturers, before the cells travel to India to be assembled into complete, safety-tested battery packs — a mechanical, near-lossless step (around 99% yield; Dunn et al. 2021, Joule). Finished packs then go to the OEM that builds the EVs this platform routes, and are distributed out to warehouses and dealers across Rajasthan's regions — Jaipur, Jodhpur, Udaipur, and the rest. Finally, end-of-life batteries feed back into the chain through hydrometallurgical recycling, recovering roughly 72% of the lithium content (Harper et al. 2019, Nature) — real, but not full recovery, which is why new mining stays necessary even as recycling scales up.

Every yield-loss number in this twin (90% at extraction/processing, 95% at precursor manufacturing, 96% at cell manufacturing, 99% at assembly, 72% recycling recovery) is a sourced figure, not a guess — see below for how they're actually used in the simulation.

Everything the Analyze tab shows you comes from a handful of supply chain concepts working together, and all of them exist for a reason. Mass balance and yield loss is the simplest: every node in the chain only turns part of what it receives into usable output (brine extraction and processing at about 90%, precursor manufacturing at about 95%, cell manufacturing at about 96%, pack assembly at about 99%, recycling recovery at about 72% -- IEA 2022/2023, Bloomberg 2022, Dunn et al. 2021, Harper et al. 2019), so the model never lets material appear or vanish for free; it's why a capacity cut at one stage doesn't translate into an equal-sized cut downstream, and why the Analyze tab's numbers are trustworthy rather than just illustrative. On top of that, every node reorders from its supplier using a base-stock policy with safety stock, sized against its own demand, its own lead time, and the service level it's targeting, and orders are smoothed rather than jumping straight to the target level (the APIOBPCS rule, Dejonckheere, Disney, Lambrecht & Towill 2003) -- this is what gives you a realistic, gradually-adjusting inventory picture instead of a chain that overreacts to every blip, and it's what the live policy table below is actually showing. That same local, imperfect-information behavior is also what produces the bullwhip effect -- order variability amplifying as it moves upstream (Lee, Padmanabhan & Whang 1997) -- which matters because it's the reason a small, short-lived shock near the customer end can still cause a large, long-lived swing further up the chain. To actually create a shock to study, the model uses disruption modeling: a generic spec that temporarily cuts one parameter of one node or shipment link for a fixed duration and then restores it, compared against a matched baseline run with the same random demand, so any difference you see is attributable to the disruption itself and not to noise. Because any single run is just one roll of the dice, the Analyze tab also runs the same scenario many times with different, realistic random demand -- Monte Carlo risk analysis -- so instead of one number you get a believable range, including how bad the worst-case trials get, which is the whole point of the risk-analysis card you see after running a scenario. A separate centralized optimization benchmark (a linear program solved with PuLP) then asks what the best possible outcome would be if one planner had perfect visibility and foresight over the whole chain, under the same physical limits -- comparing that to the simulated result tells you how much of the shortfall is simply the cost of nodes making decentralized decisions with incomplete information, rather than the disruption itself. Finally, because demand isn't one single national number, regional demand disaggregation splits it across Rajasthan's real regions, weighted by each region's actual warehouse capacity, which is exactly why the map on the Analyze tab can tell you which specific region would be hit hardest rather than only giving you one national average.
