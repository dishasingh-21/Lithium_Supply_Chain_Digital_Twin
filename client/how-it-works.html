Theory

Lithium Supply Chain Digital Twin

Every EV battery starts as salty water in Chile and ends up in a car in Rajasthan. This project builds a computer copy of that journey so we can test “what if something goes wrong?” without waiting for it to happen in real life.

Built on: EV Route Optimizer + Charging Station Network

1.What is a digital twin?

Think of it like a flight simulator, but for a supply chain. Pilots practise engine failures in a simulator so they know what to do in a real plane. In the same way, we can “break” a part of the lithium chain in the twin — a mine shuts down, a ship is delayed — and watch what happens to battery supply weeks later.

A normal dashboard only shows what already happened. A digital twin can also show what will probably happen next. To do that it needs three things:

01 / STATE

A copy of today

It knows how much stock each factory holds and what is on the way right now.

02 / BEHAVIOR

Rules for tomorrow

Simple maths rules that predict how stock, orders and shipments will change next week.

03 / FEEDBACK

Decisions go back

What we learn from the twin (e.g. “order earlier”) can be used to change the real chain.

The lithium crosses three continents before it reaches a car. Here is each step in one line:

  1. Extraction (Chile) — Salty underground water (brine) is pumped into ponds and left in the sun for months until the lithium is concentrated. A drought here slows everything.
  2. Processing (China) — The concentrate is refined into battery-grade lithium. About two-thirds of the world’s lithium is refined in China, so this is the biggest single risk point.
  3. Cell manufacturing (China) — The refined lithium is turned into cathode material and then into individual battery cells.
  4. Pack assembly (India) — Cells are put together into complete, safety-tested battery packs.
  5. OEM & regions (Rajasthan) — The car maker fits the packs into EVs, which are sent to warehouses and dealers in Jaipur, Jodhpur, Udaipur and other regions.
  6. Recycling — Old batteries are recycled and some lithium goes back into the chain. We get back only about 72%, so new mining is still needed.
Some material is lost at every step. Out of 100 kg going in, roughly 90 kg survives extraction & processing, 95 kg survives precursor making, 96 kg survives cell making and 99 kg survives pack assembly. Recycling recovers about 72 kg. These numbers come from published research (sources at the bottom of the page).

3.The ideas behind the Analyze tab

The Analyze tab uses a few standard supply chain ideas. Each card below explains one idea in plain words and why it matters for the results you see.

Idea 1

Mass balance & yield loss

Material can’t appear from nowhere or disappear for free. Each stage only turns part of its input into output (the percentages above).

Why it matters: a 20% cut at one stage doesn’t mean exactly 20% fewer batteries at the end — the losses add up along the way.

Idea 2

Reorder rule & safety stock

Each stage keeps a target stock level, like a shop that refills its shelves. It also keeps a little extra (safety stock) in case demand is higher than expected or a shipment is late. Orders are adjusted gradually, not all at once.

Why it matters: it gives a realistic picture of stock rising and falling, and it’s what the table at the bottom of this page shows.

Idea 3

Bullwhip effect

A small change in customer demand becomes a bigger change in orders at each step up the chain — like cracking a whip, where a small flick of the wrist makes a big wave at the tip.

Why it matters: a small, short shock near the customer can cause a big, long swing at the mine and refinery.

Idea 4

Disruption scenarios

We “break” one part of the chain for a while (e.g. cut refinery capacity by half for 8 weeks), then fix it. We run the same weeks again with nothing broken and compare the two.

Why it matters: because everything else is identical, any difference is caused by the disruption, not by chance.

Idea 5

Monte Carlo risk analysis

Real demand is random, so one run is like one roll of a dice. We run the same scenario many times with different random demand and look at the full range of results.

Why it matters: you see the typical outcome and how bad the worst cases can get — that’s the risk card on the Analyze tab.

Idea 6

Best-case benchmark

We also calculate the best possible result if one planner could see the whole chain and knew the future (solved with linear programming in PuLP).

Why it matters: the gap between this and the simulation shows how much loss comes from stages not sharing information, not from the disruption itself.

Idea 7

Regional demand split

Demand isn’t one number for the whole state. We split it across Rajasthan’s regions based on how much warehouse space each region has.

Why it matters: the map on the Analyze tab can tell you which region gets hit hardest, not just a single average.
